# Personal Reflection (draft)

When I started Part II, I already had a conclusion in my head.

My design was a two-layer architecture: the LLM only proposes a target speed and a time headway, and IDM still executes the actual acceleration. The idea seemed obvious — the model makes the decisions, the controller keeps it safe, and the combination should be better than either alone. The first full comparison seemed to confirm it: the LLM variant showed higher throughput and shorter travel times. I wrote that into the report draft. I had even decided how to frame it.

Looking back, I was not measuring. I was collecting evidence. The simulator was a tool for **proving**, not yet a tool for **measuring**.

The first turn was not glorious. I did not find the problem through insight; I found it by putting the "entered" columns of the three controllers side by side and noticing that the Mock column was short by about 88 vehicles on average — those vehicles never entered the road. Fewer cars on the road, so of course traffic flowed better. The "+9.5% throughput" in my draft was not a control effect; the two groups were no longer facing the same demand at all.

Fixing this took a long time, but the hard part was not the code. The hard part was deleting a paragraph I had already written. After rebuilding the comparison on a shared arrival schedule with per-seed paired differences, the advantage flipped completely: measured over all scheduled vehicles, Mock was 2.9 to 8.9 seconds slower than IDM, in all 8 seeds. What I learned is that **withdrawing** a conclusion is much harder than reaching one — because what you withdraw is not data, it is something you already said.

The second turn was metric definitions. I realised the efficiency metric only counted completed vehicles — counting only the winners — so I added a second measure: cumulative system time over all scheduled demand, truncated at the time horizon. I also found that the hard-brake "counts" were actually sample points: the same vehicle was counted 30 times within 3 seconds, because every 0.1 s sample was being counted. After switching to event-based counting, the conclusion changed from "more frequent" to "same frequency, longer each time" (626 vs 638 events; 169.8 s vs 593.5 s total duration). Same data, two statements, both "supported by data" — but only one of them is true. During my internship as a product manager, what I feared most was good-looking numbers with the wrong definition. This time I stepped on that same rake myself, deeper than I expected.

The third turn was the real-model experiment. My offline estimate said about 216 API calls; the actual number was 189 — close in total, but the distribution was completely different: the long-bottleneck scenarios triggered more than twice as many calls as the short ones, because the model's own decisions changed the trigger sequence. An offline estimate is a budget, not a conclusion. And the real result: the Qwen-driven vehicle was 3.6 seconds slower at the median and 102.3 seconds slower in the worst run; there was no collision, but the minimum TTC dropped to 0.76 s once. I had hoped it would at least not lose. It lost. Strangely, those 189 calls are my favourite numbers in the whole report — they are the only numbers that left me no room for imagination.

After these three turns, the original claim that "the LLM is better" shrank to one sentence: across the 96 controlled runs, Mock held a larger safety margin — exposure to TTC under 3 seconds fell from 1069.8 to 7.5 pair-seconds — at the price of being slower and braking longer. That is not "better". That is a trade. I could write that sentence only because I had deleted all the louder ones before it.

AI played a different role this time. In Part I it was mostly an executor, and I judged its output; this time it also acted as a reviewer, and it flagged several of the errors first. That did not change the nature of the work: it can flag an error, but whether to withdraw the conclusion was still my decision. At the end of Part I, I concluded that the human role is to define, to judge, and to take responsibility. Part II taught me that the hardest of the three is judging — especially when the object of judgement is not the AI's output, but a conclusion I had already written down myself.

What this course changed is not my model. It is the way I draw conclusions: first ask whether the data can carry the sentence, then decide whether to write it down.
